Pocha nights: soju, beer and the pour that means respect

A tarp, four plastic stools, one owner and a table that gets louder by the hour. Korean drinking has rules — and almost all of them are about taking care of the person next to you.

You have seen it in every Korean drama: the orange tent, the plastic stools, the owner flipping something on a grill, and two people clinking glasses under a bare bulb. That is a 포차 (pocha) — short for 포장마차, literally “a wagon that can be wrapped up”.

It is also the hardest social situation in Korea to walk into without a script, because the language is only half of it. The other half is where your hands go, when you pour, and how you say no without anyone losing face. This lesson covers both.

What a pocha is

Pochas started as mobile stalls that could be folded away before the morning, and they still feel like that: a tarp, a frame, a gas burner and a menu written in marker. You will find them near markets, around university districts, along the Cheonggyecheon stream, and in nightlife areas where the restaurants have already closed.

Inside there are no reservations, no host and usually no menu in English. You sit where there is space, you call the owner when you need something, and you pay at the end. Prices in tourist-heavy areas can be higher than the same food two streets away, so the prices are usually written on the tarp or a board — glance at them before you sit down.

Calling the owner is not rude

In Korea, calling 사장님! (sajangnim — “boss”) across a room is normal and polite. Waiting quietly for service is the thing that looks strange. What you should not do is wave money or snap your fingers — that reads as looking down on the person serving you.

Getting a table and ordering

The first exchange is short. You need to know whether they have space, how many of you there are, and what to eat with the drinks. Everything here is said at normal volume, over noise — so keep it to a few words and repeat if you must.

The pour: five rules

This is where visitors get nervous, and the good news is that the rules are simple and everyone can see you are trying. Nobody expects a foreigner to be perfect. They do notice two hands.

Rule one: you do not pour your own glass. Someone pours for you; you pour for them. If your glass is empty and nobody is looking, you can pour for the person next to you first, and they will usually return it.

Rule two: two hands on the bottle. Right hand on the bottle, left hand lightly supporting your right forearm (or resting at your chest). When you receive, hold the glass with two hands, or support your right wrist with your left.

Rule three: turn away from a senior. If the person you are drinking with is older or more senior at work, turn your head slightly to the side when you drink rather than facing them directly. It reads as respect, not rudeness, and Koreans do it without thinking.

Rule four: wait for the eldest. At a mixed table, nobody lifts a glass first. Watch who starts, then match.

Rule five: an empty glass is an invitation. Leaving a little in your glass signals “I am finished”. If the bottle is already coming towards you, cover the glass with one hand and say the line below.

Anju, and why you eat while drinking

Koreans rarely drink on an empty table. 안주 exists partly because drinking is a shared, slow activity — something to do with your hands — and partly because food slows the alcohol down. A table with bottles and no plates looks, to a Korean eye, like a problem.

The classics you will see on every pocha tarp: 계란말이 (rolled omelette, the safest choice and always good), 오뎅탕 (fish cake soup, served in a paper cup or a pot), 골뱅이무침 (spicy whelk salad, chewy and very Korean), 닭발 (chicken feet — the texture is the point), 감자전 (potato pancake) and, at the colder end of the year, anything on a grill.

If you do not drink at all

Order 사이다 (cider, a clear lemon-lime soda), 콜라 or 식혜 (a sweet rice drink), and hold the glass like everyone else. Someone will almost certainly say 한 잔만 — “just one glass”. You are allowed to say no. 알레르기가 있어요 (“I’m allergic”) ends the topic immediately and is not a lie if it is true.

Paying, and the second round

The bill arrives at the table only if you ask. Otherwise you walk up to the counter — and in Korea, paying is a social act, sometimes a small competition. If you genuinely want to treat the table, say it early and go pay before the plates are cleared. Offering to split is completely normal among younger people and colleagues of the same level.

Culture deep dive: soju, rounds and saying no

소맥 is a ratio, not a drink

소맥 (somaek) is soju plus beer — 소 from soju, 맥 from maekju. The usual mix is around three parts soju to seven parts beer, which lands near wine strength and goes down far too easily. A five-to-five mix is the version people regret. The more theatrical cousin, 폭탄주 (bomb shot), is a shot glass of soju dropped into a glass of beer — watch it once, then decide.

건배, and the toast that has nothing to do with you

건배 is the everyday clink. 위하여 — literally “for this” — is the formal toast shouted in unison at company dinners, usually after the most senior person says a few words. If you are at a 회식 (company dinner) and everyone suddenly raises a glass and shouts one word, this is it. Raise your glass and join in; nobody will ask you to make a speech.

The oldest person sets the tempo

Korean drinking culture still runs on age order, even as it loosens. The eldest pours first, drinks first, and often decides when the table ends. This is not servitude — the same structure means the eldest is also expected to pay and to look after everyone. If you are the oldest at the table, you will be expected to lead; if you are the youngest, pouring for others is a small, easy way to be liked.

Saying no without saying no

Direct refusals are rare at a Korean table because the whole ritual is about inclusion. The polite toolkit, in order of subtlety:

  • Leave a little in your glass. Everyone can read it.
  • Cover the glass with your hand and say 괜찮아요.
  • Say 저는 술을 못 마셔요 once, clearly, and order something else to hold.
  • Leave early and warmly: 저는 이만 갈게요, 오늘 재밌었어요 — “I’ll head off, this was fun”.

And a note that belongs in a lesson rather than a travel blog: Korea’s drinking culture is real and it is also changing. Younger Koreans drink less, “no alcohol” tables are normal, and nobody is obliged to keep up. The etiquette is about warmth, not volume.

Before you go

Two practical notes. Pochas close when the owner decides, not at a set time, and the last train in Seoul runs earlier than the last bottle — check the time before the second round. And if you are the guest, offer to pay once. In Korea the offer itself is the courtesy, and being refused is part of the ritual, not a rejection.
